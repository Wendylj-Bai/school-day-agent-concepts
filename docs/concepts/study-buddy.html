Concept 02 · Learning support with people involved

Personalized Study Buddy

Help a learner move forward after a practice mistake while giving the learner, authorized parents, and educators an appropriate view of progress, support needs, and next steps.

Recurring practice needTwo limited agent rolesRole-based progress check-ins
Working hypothesis for team discussion. The technology supports learning; it does not replace a teacher, detach parents from the learner, diagnose a child, or prove learning impact.
View the UI concept
Problem and pain

A learner can remain stuck when timely, appropriate help is unavailable.

A fictional learner answers 52 − 27 as 35, subtracting the smaller digit from the larger one in each place instead of regrouping. A static answer key shows the result but cannot explain the misunderstanding. A general chatbot may simply provide the homework answer. The example is illustrative, while the research below documents both the broader mathematics-support problem and this specific error pattern.

Core problem

The learner needs help at the moment of difficulty

Students may struggle outside school hours when a teacher or a parent with subject knowledge is not immediately available.

Pain experienced

Repeated mistakes can become frustration

A general explanation may not address the misunderstanding. Busy parents may want to help but may not know which explanation to try, while teachers cannot provide individual support at every moment.

How the AI could help

Review, teach, check, and adjust within limits

The Learning Review Agent notices a possible learning difficulty from practice attempts. The Teaching Agent selects a teacher-approved hint or activity, presents a new question, and tries another method when the answer check shows that the learner is still struggling.

Why use agents?

The next step depends on what just happened

The system must review an attempt, choose an appropriate method, check whether it worked, adjust within a retry limit, and involve an authorized adult when needed. A fixed quiz cannot complete that learning cycle.

Value for each participant

Student value

Receive a hint matched to the attempt, practice independently, and ask for help without being handed the answer.

Parent value

See a short, authorized progress and support summary, then encourage practice or help arrange follow-up.

Teacher and adviser value

Approve learning activities, guide lesson review, and receive a useful signal when continued difficulty needs human attention.

Shared accountability

Show useful progress without turning support into surveillance.

The Study Buddy records observable practice milestones and shares only what each authorized participant needs.

Learner view

Shows the detailed plan, feedback, completed activities, support requested, and the next task.

Authorized parent view

Shows a high-level signal such as 3 of 5 activities completed, on track, or support requested. It does not automatically expose every mistake, the full AI conversation, or private educator notes.

Teacher or adviser view

Shows academic evidence, recurring difficulty, support used, and items needing follow-up. The educator decides whether practice demonstrates mastery.

Completion is not mastery

The system may report that an activity was completed or a no-hint check was passed. Only an authorized educator can confirm curriculum mastery or make an academic decision.

A real deployment would require age-appropriate consent, school policy, and role-based access. The first prototype uses fictional learners and fixed demo permissions.
Proposed agent design

Separate learning review from teaching, then reconnect the learner with people.

The two roles create a visible learning cycle while fixed application rules control correct answers, limits, and who may see the results.

Agent 1

Learning Review Agent

Reviews current and recent practice attempts, records a possible learning difficulty with supporting evidence, and identifies the next practice goal. It does not diagnose or grade the learner.

Agent 2

Teaching Agent

Selects a teacher-approved activity or visual, gives a hint rather than the answer, and presents a different practice question. After an unsuccessful check, it selects one other approved method.

Fixed application rules

The AI does not decide correctness, limits, or sharing.

The application checks every response, including each new practice answer. It also controls retries, session length, allowed content, answer leakage, and what progress information each authorized person may see.

Human participation

The cycle ends with encouragement or appropriate follow-up.

Parents can encourage the learner and help with study routines. Teachers or advisers approve content, review evidence, and decide whether practice demonstrates mastery. Continued difficulty produces a support signal, not an automatic diagnosis.

Practice and mock-test mode

Prepare independently, then adjust the next practice plan.

The Study Buddy can create a short quiz or mock test from a teacher-approved question bank. During the attempt, hints and answers stay locked so the result reflects independent practice rather than assisted performance. It works in two ways: as the final check at the end of a practice cycle, as shown in the workflow below, or as a standalone mode the learner starts on their own.

Before the mock test

The learner selects an approved skill and difficulty. The system creates new practice questions without copying a real assessment.

During the attempt

No hints, answer reveals, or coaching. Fixed answer rules record and score questions with clear right or wrong answers after submission.

After submission

The agents explain missed topics using approved material, recommend focused practice, and create a new follow-up quiz. The result is a practice-readiness summary, not an official grade or predicted test score.

Preparation and practice only. The system must not assist during a live, graded, or restricted assessment.

How to read this flow

A practice mistake begins a controlled learning and support cycle.

The agents help choose an explanation and next step. Fixed application rules control correct answers, retry limits, approved content, and who may see the learner’s support summary.

  1. The learner attempts a practice question. The system begins with what the learner actually tried, not with a general request for the homework answer.
  2. Fixed answer rules mark every response. This includes the first attempt and each new practice answer. The AI does not decide whether an answer is correct.
  3. The Learning Review Agent considers where the learner may need help. It records a possible learning difficulty supported by the attempts. It does not diagnose the learner or assign an unofficial grade.
  4. The Teaching Agent selects an approved way to help. It provides a hint or activity and then asks a different practice question, which fixed rules check again.
  5. The system adjusts or involves people. If the new answer is still incorrect, Try Another Method sends the Teaching Agent back with one different approved method. Once the learner answers correctly, a short mock quiz without hints checks whether they can work independently. After the mock quiz, or when the retry limit is reached, the system prepares a short summary for an authorized parent, teacher, or adviser. The adult decides what follow-up is appropriate.
Example seven-minute demo

Show adjustment, accountability, and participation—not an isolated chatbot.

  1. A fictional student answers 52 − 27 as 35, a common mistake when subtracting two-digit numbers that need regrouping.
  2. Fixed answer rules mark the attempt incorrect. The Learning Review Agent records a possible regrouping difficulty and asks how the learner got the 5 in the ones place before labeling it.
  3. The Teaching Agent selects a teacher-approved place-value activity (trade one ten for ten ones) and presents a new check question: 61 − 38.
  4. The student answers 37, so the fixed rules mark it incorrect. Try Another Method sends the Teaching Agent back to a number-line approach (count up from 38: +2 to 40, +20 to 60, +1 to 61, so 23) within the one-retry limit.
  5. The student answers correctly, then takes a short mock quiz without hints. The learner view marks the practice activity complete and shows the next task. A fictional parent view shows a high-level 3 of 5 activities completed signal, while the teacher view shows the attempts, support used, and mock-quiz result for review. The system does not declare mastery.
Evidence that the problem and adoption are happening

Students need mathematics support, and many teens already use general AI for schoolwork.

The evidence validates the education problem, the specific subtraction mistake in our demo, limits on help at home, and existing student adoption of AI. It does not claim that this particular Study Buddy already improves learning.

Mathematics difficulty is widespread

The 2024 Nation's Report Card found that 24% of US fourth-grade students performed below NAEP Basic in mathematics, while 39% performed at or above NAEP Proficient. This is broad national evidence of uneven performance, not evidence that every student needs an AI tutor.

Our demo error is documented

A 2020 Frontiers study describes the same “smaller-from-larger” subtraction error used in our demo and notes that it has appeared across educational settings. It also warns that one wrong answer may be a simple slip, supporting our rule to ask about the learner’s reasoning.

Parents cannot always fill the gap

In a 2014 survey, 60.1% of parents with children in grades K–8 reported difficulty helping with homework. Reasons included not understanding the subject (33.5%) and being too busy (25.5%). This is older supporting evidence, not a current estimate.

Students already use AI for schoolwork

A 2026 Pew survey found that 54% of US teens had used AI chatbots for schoolwork, including about four in ten or more who used them for math problems or research. One in ten said chatbots helped with all or most of their schoolwork.

Why a guided approach matters

A Penn/Wharton experiment found that general GPT improved assisted practice but students performed worse than the control group when AI was removed. This supports hints and later checks without AI assistance; it does not prove our product works.

Human-supported AI has shown promise

In Stanford's Tutor CoPilot trial, students whose human tutors used AI support were 4 percentage points more likely to master math topics. This supports keeping people involved, but it did not test a child using an AI tutor alone at home.

What the evidence establishes

Learning difficulties occur, parents cannot always provide immediate subject help, and students are already turning to general AI. These findings support testing a controlled agent that gives hints, changes approved teaching methods, checks independent practice, and involves adults.

Evidence boundary

The parent survey is from 2014, Pew studied teens rather than younger children, and the tutoring studies tested different settings. They support the problem and adoption context, not a measured benefit for this Study Buddy.

Research-backed design rule: An answer such as 52 − 27 = 35 suggests, but does not prove, a regrouping difficulty. The agent should ask how the learner reasoned before choosing a teaching method.

Sources: Nation's Report Card, 2024 · Frontiers in Education, 2020 · NCFL, 2014 · Pew Research Center, 2026 · Knowledge at Wharton, 2024 · Stanford Tutor CoPilot, 2025

Suggested first prototype · not implemented

One skill, a small approved content library, and one support cycle.

One narrow skill

Two-digit subtraction with regrouping, planned mistake examples, fictional student profiles, new check questions, a short mock quiz, a learner progress view, and one fictional authorized-adult view. A full parent portal is outside the first scope.

Possible tools

LangGraph to manage the steps; MCP for controlled access to approved activities and hints; fixed answer rules; Base44 or a custom screen; Antigravity to help with development.

Measures

Performance on new post-hint and mock-test questions, answer leakage, appropriate method changes, retry limits followed, accurate milestones, role-appropriate sharing, useful support signals, and timely human follow-up.
